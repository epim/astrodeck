Weather awareness

See which half of your sky is worth pointing at.

Forty per cent cloud tells you nothing about where the gap is. AstroDeck shades your whole hemisphere with real satellite cloud, so you can look once and know: the east is socked in, the west is open, and the bank moving through will reach your target in about forty minutes.

The geometry

Why the cloud overhead does not matter.

Point at 40° with a deck at 3 km and the cloud that blocks you sits three and a half kilometres downrange. Read the pixel above your observatory and you have measured somebody else's sky.

On the Monitor screen

Pick a target that stays clear.

Horizon to zenith, shaded with the last satellite pass, your scope marked where it points. Before you commit three hours to something you can see whether it sits under the gap or under the bank.

The dome
  • Ask about any patch of sky. Give it an altitude and azimuth and get a number back, with the reading it came from.
  • And about ten minutes from now. Two satellite passes give the cloud field a speed and a bearing, so you can ask where it will be by the time you get there.
  • It says when it does not know. A missing cloud-top height comes back as unknown. If no wind level matches the drift it measured, it tells you that too.
  • It says when it cannot see you. Outside the satellite footprint you get told, rather than an empty blue dome that means nothing.

Three sources

Three sources, and they check each other.

They disagree sometimes, which is how you catch a forecast that has already been overtaken by the sky outside.

Hours ahead

Forecast

Open-Meteo, or Astrospheric with your own key. Layered cloud, seeing, transparency, dewpoint and wind. This is what decides whether tonight is worth arming, and what stops an unattended rig unparking into overcast.

Minutes ahead

Satellite

NOAA GOES-18 and GOES-19, straight from the public archive, refreshing every five minutes. Cloud mask and cloud-top height, projected through the real satellite geometry so the dome matches the sky you are standing under.

Right now

Your own frames

AstroDeck measures bright-star count and contrast on every sub. This one cannot be wrong about your sky, because it is a photograph of it, and this is the one that stops a run.

The satellite never closes your observatory. It shows you where the cloud is; your own frames decide whether the sky is worth exposing. A five-minute-old satellite pass does not get to shut down a rig that can still see stars.

Reactions

What it does when the sky turns.

WhenWhat happensHow it comes back
Cloud arrives Capture holds. Tracking continues, the guider parks, and hold darks can be shot while waiting. Releases itself once the sky clears for your set duration, then re-cools, re-centres with a solve and refocuses if HFR drifted.
Hold runs long Your maximum hold expires. Whatever you chose: keep waiting, or park and close.
Forecast is bad at dusk Auto-resume vetoes the night rather than unparking into overcast. Re-checks on its own cadence. "Ignore weather tonight" overrides it.
Safety monitor unsafe Abort. Park, close, warm. A stale sensor reading counts as unsafe. Manual. This one never releases itself.
Frames go bad The quality gate rejects them and the ledger records why. Rejected frames are re-shot. Nothing is silently lost from the count.

Where it works

The satellite half is the Americas.

GOES-18 and GOES-19 sit over the Americas. North and South America get the dome, sharpest under the satellite and coarser toward the edge of its view. Europe, Africa, Asia and Oceania fall outside it.

Set up a site there and AstroDeck tells you so. It does not draw an empty sky you might read as clear, and it stops downloading, because the answer will not change.

Everything else works anywhere: the forecast, the safety monitor, and the cloud detection running on your own frames.

Cloud data courtesy NOAA GOES on AWS. Forecast from Open-Meteo, or Astrospheric with your own key.
