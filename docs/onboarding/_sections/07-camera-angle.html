Framing the Shot

Camera angle, where rotation becomes composition

The telescope decides what you point at. The rotation of the camera behind it decides how that thing sits in the frame, and those are not the same decision.

A camera sensor is a rectangle, and the sky does not care which way you hold it. Spin the camera and the exact same galaxy can streak corner to corner like it has somewhere to be, or lie down flat across the middle looking faintly bored. Nothing about the target changed. You just rotated the rectangle. That angle has a name, the position angle, and it is as much a part of the photograph as exposure or focus.

There are two ways to set it. You can loosen a clamp, turn the camera with your hands in the dark like you are adjusting a stubborn doorknob, and lock it down close to right. Or a motorized rotator drives the camera to a number you asked for. Either way you are never quite sure you nailed it until the sky tells you, and the sky tells you through plate-solving, which reads an exposure and reports the angle the camera actually landed at, down to a fraction of a degree.

That readout matters most when you are stitching a mosaic, several framed tiles meant to line up into one larger picture. If panel three is rotated two degrees off from panels one and two, the seam shows, and no amount of later wrangling fully hides a crooked join. Planned angle and actual angle need to agree.

So when you reach the rotation control in AstroDeck, the question worth asking is how clearly it shows the gap between the angle you asked for and the angle you actually got. A single number is a setting. Two numbers side by side, with the difference called out, is a tool you can actually steer by.
