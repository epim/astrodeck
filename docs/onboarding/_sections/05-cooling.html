The Sensor's Self-Inflicted Lies

Cold sensors lie less

A camera sensor is supposed to report the light that lands on it. The embarrassing secret is that it also reports light that never arrived — a faint static it generates entirely on its own, just by being a warm electronic thing. That self-made signal is called dark current, and on a long night-sky exposure it speckles the frame with noise the sky never put there.

Two things make it worse, and both are unavoidable in this hobby: heat and time. The warmer the sensor, the faster it lies; the longer the exposure, the more of those lies pile up. Since the whole point of astrophotography is staring at one faint thing for a very long time, you can't shorten the exposures — so you attack the temperature instead.

+20° −10° set −30°
+18 °C sensor
cooling… at set-point

In words, for anyone the animation skips: the grainy frame settles into a clean one as the thermometer falls from +18°C to its −10°C set-point, and the status flips from a pulsing amber "cooling" to a steady green "at set-point."

The fix is a little thermoelectric cooler bolted to the sensor that holds it at a chosen set-point — say −10°C — and keeps it there all night. The set-point matters more than the raw coldness. You later subtract reference "dark" frames to cancel the leftover speckle, and that subtraction only works if the darks were shot at the same temperature. A stable, repeatable −10°C is worth more than a colder number you can't hit twice.

Cooling is a deliberate process with a real finish line, and it can't always reach whatever number you typed in — which is exactly why the indicator has to be honest about where it actually is.

For the AstroDeck UI, the cooler box lives or dies on one question the user asks over and over: is it cooling, is it at the set-point, or is it off? That state needs to be readable at a glance and honest about the in-between — "cooling" is a process with a finish line, and the indicator should make the finish line obvious, not leave the user squinting at a temperature number doing long division in their head.
