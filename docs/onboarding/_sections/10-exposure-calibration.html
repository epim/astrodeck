Stacking & Calibration

Signal is shy. You coax it out with arithmetic.

A single photo of a galaxy is mostly an honest portrait of your sensor having a bad night. The thing you came for is buried in the grain, barely above it. The fix is not a better single shot. It is a lot of shots, added together with care.

Here is the whole idea in one sentence: the random speckle we call noise is different in every frame, so when you average many frames it partly cancels itself out, while the real light from the sky is in the same place every time and stacks up. Take four times as many frames and the noise drops by about half. Take a hundred and the faint stuff you couldn't even see starts to surface like a photograph developing in a tray.

Stacking averages away the random mess. It does nothing about the errors that show up identically in every frame, and there are three of those worth knowing by name. The move is to photograph your sensor's predictable mistakes once and subtract them from every real frame. The astronomers call these calibration frames, which sounds more ceremonial than it is.

  • Lights
    The actual pictures of the sky. Everything else exists to clean these up.
  • Darks
    Shots with the lens capped. They map the heat glow the sensor makes on its own, so you can subtract it.
  • Flats
    Every lens is a little brighter in the middle and dimmer at the edges, and stray dust on the glass throws soft shadows. A flat is a photo of an evenly lit blank that maps exactly that unevenness, so dividing it out makes the frame uniform again.

    • None of this is glamorous. It is bookkeeping, the kind a careful accountant would respect. But it is the entire difference between a brown smudge and a clean image, and it is the sort of thing software should be quietly handling for you rather than making you remember.

    Where this lands in AstroDeck: the exposure and sequence settings are where someone decides how long each frame runs and how many to collect, and the calibration plan is where darks and flats get scheduled or pulled from a library. If a person has to keep that arithmetic in their own head at 2am, the interface is doing too little. The good version makes the trade visible: more frames, less noise, here's roughly where you are on that curve.
