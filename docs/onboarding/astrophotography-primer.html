The Premise

You are about to photograph a faint smudge from a spinning rock

The thing you want a picture of is dim, absurdly far away, and was last interesting to the universe a few million years ago. To capture it, you hold the shutter open for minutes — sometimes hours — and let the light dribble in. The catch is that you are standing on a planet that refuses to stop turning, so your target is sliding across the sky the entire time you're trying to hold still on it.

That drift is the whole problem in one image. Everything that follows is built to cancel it.

So most of the craft is a quiet fight against three things: motion (the sky won't sit still), noise (faint signal buried in sensor static), and focus (a star is a point, and points are unforgiving). To win those fights you bolt together a stack of boxes called the imaging train. Here it is at a glance — the three that do the work, and two helpers you'll meet later.

Core Mount The robot arm. Aims at the target and tracks it as the sky slides.
Core Telescope The lens, called an OTA. A very long, very patient camera lens.
Core Main camera The sensor that actually collects the photons. The whole point.
Helper Guide scope + cam A tiny second eye that watches one star and nudges the mount.
Helper Focuser + filters Pulls focus to a hair, and swaps color filters in front of the sensor.
Does the work Makes the work better

None of these boxes were designed to talk to each other. Historically you'd run a different app for each one, alt-tabbing between five programs at two in the morning in the cold. AstroDeck is the brain that drives the whole train from one screen — so the rest of this guide is really a tour of the things that brain has to coordinate, and a chance for you to decide where its controls belong.

Pointing the Mount

The sky moves, so the mount moves back

There's a beautiful cheat hiding in the geometry. The sky only appears to turn because the Earth is turning underneath it, around one fixed axis. Match that axis with one of the mount's axes, spin a single motor at exactly the right speed in the opposite direction, and the sky's motion is cancelled. A star that should be sliding off your sensor instead sits perfectly still.

That's what an equatorial mount is: a mount tilted so one of its axes is parallel to the Earth's. Once it's aligned, holding a target is no longer a frantic two-handed steering job. It's one motor, turning slowly, forever, at what's called the sidereal rate — roughly one full revolution a day. Watch what that does. The frame starts drifting the way the real sky drifts; flip tracking on and one star locks to the crosshair while everything else quietly stops sliding.

The amber star is the one you asked the mount to hold. With tracking on, it stops dead — and so does every other star, because they're all carried by the same single motor.

To tell the mount which point to hold, you give it two numbers. They work just like latitude and longitude, but painted onto the dome of the sky and rotating with it, so they don't change as the night goes on.

RA — Right Ascension

Sky-longitude

How far around the celestial sphere a target sits — a fixed label that names the target and stays put all night. The RA-axis motor is the one turning at sidereal rate, moving constantly to keep that fixed number fixed.

Dec — Declination

Sky-latitude

How far north or south of the celestial equator a target sits. Once you're on target, this barely moves.

Tracking is excellent and never quite perfect — a touch of mechanical slop here, a hair of misalignment there, and over a long exposure that star drifts a few pixels anyway. That small, stubborn error is exactly why guiding exists, which is a story for later. For now the thing to hold onto is the control surface: slew to point at something, track on/off to hold it, and go-to to send the mount to a named target by its coordinates. Those three live in AstroDeck's mount panel, and the test is whether you can read the mount's state at a glance — pointed where, holding or not — without hunting for it.

Aiming the Spin Axis

Polar alignment: aim the axis at the pole, or everything smears

The whole cancel-the-sky trick from a moment ago has one precondition: the mount's spinning axis has to point at the exact pivot the sky turns around — the celestial pole, which up north sits a whisker away from Polaris. Aim there and the sky holds. Aim a little off, and the mount spends all night turning around the wrong center, smearing your stars in a way no single exposure can undo.

What goes wrong, while the mount is merely tracking, is drift. The motor turns around the wrong center, so the whole field slides steadily in one direction, and every star smears into a short, nearly straight streak — all the streaks roughly parallel, like rain on a windscreen. The longer the exposure, the longer the streak. Here are the two outcomes side by side, each star drawing its own path over a few minutes — parallel smears on the left, held points on the right.

Same sky, same exposure. The only difference is where the spin axis is pointed.

Later you'll meet a guider, a second camera that pins one star and erases this drift in real time. It does a remarkable job, which raises a fair question: if a guider cancels the smear, why bother aligning at all? Because the guider can only nail one star. With the axis still off, the rest of the field slowly rotates around that pinned point over a long night — the residual that survives guiding. Good polar alignment is what keeps that rotation small enough to ignore. So you still aim the axis carefully, and you don't do it by eyeballing Polaris through a hole, at least not anymore. A routine — three-point, or a drift test — has the camera photograph the sky, plate-solves those frames — reads the star pattern to work out exactly where the scope is pointing — to figure out precisely where your axis sits, and then tells you the error: which direction, and how far. You correct it with two physical knobs on the mount, altitude (up/down) and azimuth (left/right). The classic way to show all this is a bullseye. Your error is a dot off-center; your job is to turn the knobs and walk that dot into the middle.

The direction cues have to say turn this knob, this way in language a tired beginner trusts, rather than quietly assuming you already know which way azimuth runs. And the "you're done" moment has to be unmistakable. So here's the question worth carrying into the screen: could you correct your alignment with this thing while wearing gloves and resenting the cold? That's where AstroDeck's bullseye earns its keep — prime territory for an opinion.

Sharpness, Measured

A star should be a point, not a marshmallow

A real star is unfathomably far away, which means that for our purposes it has no size at all. The job of focus is to make the camera agree: to collapse each star down to the smallest, brightest dot the optics can manage. Miss focus and that same star bloats into a soft, pale blob. Same star, same sky, worse photo.

The trap is that a slightly soft star and a perfectly sharp one look nearly identical to a human at 2am, so we stop trusting eyeballs and start trusting a number. The usual one is HFR — half-flux radius — and you can read it as "how fat is this star, in pixels." Smaller is sharper. The entire game is to drive that number down and keep it there.

One inconvenient detail keeps this from being a one-and-done: focus drifts. As the night cools, the telescope's metal contracts by a hair, the focus point shifts, and your once-sharp stars quietly puff back up. So you refocus every so often through the night — a chore that is begging to be automated, which is exactly where we're headed: re-running that V-curve on a schedule, watching the HFR number, and nudging the focuser without a human in the loop.

When you look at AstroDeck's focus panel, the thing to hold onto is that the HFR readout is the protagonist, not the buttons around it. The user is hunting a minimum, and the two flows that carry a control — a manual nudge and the full V-curve run — exist to make that number small and tell them, plainly, when it is.

The Sensor's Self-Inflicted Lies

Cold sensors lie less

A camera sensor is supposed to report the light that lands on it. The embarrassing secret is that it also reports light that never arrived — a faint static it generates entirely on its own, just by being a warm electronic thing. That self-made signal is called dark current, and on a long night-sky exposure it speckles the frame with noise the sky never put there.

Two things make it worse, and both are unavoidable in this hobby: heat and time. The warmer the sensor, the faster it lies; the longer the exposure, the more of those lies pile up. Since the whole point of astrophotography is staring at one faint thing for a very long time, you can't shorten the exposures — so you attack the temperature instead.

In words, for anyone the animation skips: the grainy frame settles into a clean one as the thermometer falls from +18°C to its −10°C set-point, and the status flips from a pulsing amber "cooling" to a steady green "at set-point."

The fix is a little thermoelectric cooler bolted to the sensor that holds it at a chosen set-point — say −10°C — and keeps it there all night. The set-point matters more than the raw coldness. You later subtract reference "dark" frames to cancel the leftover speckle, and that subtraction only works if the darks were shot at the same temperature. A stable, repeatable −10°C is worth more than a colder number you can't hit twice.

Cooling is a deliberate process with a real finish line, and it can't always reach whatever number you typed in — which is exactly why the indicator has to be honest about where it actually is.

For the AstroDeck UI, the cooler box lives or dies on one question the user asks over and over: is it cooling, is it at the set-point, or is it off? That state needs to be readable at a glance and honest about the in-between — "cooling" is a process with a finish line, and the indicator should make the finish line obvious, not leave the user squinting at a temperature number doing long division in their head.

Pointing & Composition

How much sky fits, and where the target lands

Before any of the careful stuff — focus, cooling, hours of exposure — there is a plainly creative question: what's in the shot? A telescope is a lens with a fixed crop, and your only compositional moves are how wide that crop is and where you aim it. Get those wrong and you've spent the whole night perfectly photographing the corner of something.

How much sky fits comes down to focal length against sensor size. A long focal length is a tight crop — great for a small, distant galaxy, hopeless for anything big. A short focal length is a wide field that swallows a whole sprawling nebula but renders each object small. Underneath sits pixel scale, a fineness-of-detail dial with a sweet spot in the middle; the UI's job is to keep the user inside that sweet spot, not hand them a knob they'll reflexively crank to the max.

Drag the focal-length slider and the amber frame grows wider or crops tighter over the nebula. The readout's field of view is in degrees, and a useful yardstick is the full Moon, which is about half a degree across — so a frame reading 2.3° is roughly four-and-a-half Moons wide. Press plate-solve & center and a scan line sweeps the sky, then the frame slides to put the target dead-center — the same trick the software uses to land on a target precisely instead of "roughly over there."

Aiming is the part that feels like magic and is really just bookkeeping. The mount points where it thinks the target is, the camera takes a quick frame, and the software plate-solves it — recognizes the star pattern, works out exactly where the scope is actually looking, and nudges it until the target sits where you framed it. No star-hopping, no "a little to the left." When a target is too big to fit even the widest sensible frame, you shoot it as a multi-panel mosaic and stitch the tiles together later, which is its own little composition problem in a grid.

On AstroDeck's framing screen, the sky atlas is doing two jobs at once: showing the user a true-to-scale preview of what the sensor will capture, and letting them place that rectangle on the target with intent. The interesting UX question is how a wide-field shot, a tight crop, and a multi-tile mosaic all live in the same preview without the user ever having to picture arcseconds in their head.

Framing the Shot

Camera angle, where rotation becomes composition

The telescope decides what you point at. The rotation of the camera behind it decides how that thing sits in the frame, and those are not the same decision.

A camera sensor is a rectangle, and the sky does not care which way you hold it. Spin the camera and the exact same galaxy can streak corner to corner like it has somewhere to be, or lie down flat across the middle looking faintly bored. Nothing about the target changed. You just rotated the rectangle. That angle has a name, the position angle, and it is as much a part of the photograph as exposure or focus.

There are two ways to set it. You can loosen a clamp, turn the camera with your hands in the dark like you are adjusting a stubborn doorknob, and lock it down close to right. Or a motorized rotator drives the camera to a number you asked for. Either way you are never quite sure you nailed it until the sky tells you, and the sky tells you through plate-solving, which reads an exposure and reports the angle the camera actually landed at, down to a fraction of a degree.

That readout matters most when you are stitching a mosaic, several framed tiles meant to line up into one larger picture. If panel three is rotated two degrees off from panels one and two, the seam shows, and no amount of later wrangling fully hides a crooked join. Planned angle and actual angle need to agree.

So when you reach the rotation control in AstroDeck, the question worth asking is how clearly it shows the gap between the angle you asked for and the angle you actually got. A single number is a setting. Two numbers side by side, with the difference called out, is a tool you can actually steer by.

Holding Still

Guiding, or a second camera that babysits one star

A mount that tracks the sky is close to right, and close to right is not the same as right when your shutter is open for five minutes.

The earth turns, so the sky appears to drift, so the mount slowly rotates to cancel that drift and hold the target still. The trouble is that gears have tiny imperfections and the atmosphere shoves the air around, and over a few minutes those small errors add up. A star that should be a clean round point smears into a little comma. At this scale the errors are measured in arcseconds — the same hair-thin angle from the framing section, a coin held a few miles off — and a couple of those is enough to ruin a frame.

The fix is almost suspiciously simple. A small second camera locks onto one ordinary star and watches it, several times a minute, asking the same dull question over and over: did it move? When the star slips a hair to the left, the guide camera tells the mount to nudge a hair to the right. The graph below is that conversation, the wobble of a star being kept honest by something that never blinks.

There is one deliberate exception to all this holding-still. Between exposures the system will sometimes shove the whole frame a few pixels sideways on purpose, a move called a dither (watch the box hop). It sounds like sabotage, undoing the very precision you just paid for, but the point is that a dead pixel or a fixed smudge stays put on the sensor while the stars land somewhere new each time. When the frames are later stacked, the software can tell the difference and quietly throw the junk away.

That error graph is the most data-dense surface in the entire app, and probably the most improvable. A jagged line near zero is good news, but it does not feel like good news at a glance, and a beginner reading it has no idea whether to relax or panic. Worth asking what a healthy guiding readout in AstroDeck should make you feel, not just what it should plot.

Knowing Where You Are

Plate solving, the app working out exactly where it's pointed

Point a telescope at the sky and it has no idea where it is looking. It knows where you told it to look, which is a different and more optimistic thing.

Here is the trick that fixes that, and it is genuinely clever. The system takes a quick exposure, finds the stars in it, and measures the pattern they make, the distances and angles between them. Then it searches a catalog of the whole sky for the one place where that exact pattern lives, the way you might recognize a friend from the spacing of three freckles. Star patterns are effectively unique, so once it finds the match it knows precisely where the telescope points, which way the camera is rotated, and how much sky each pixel covers. It is GPS for a telescope, built entirely out of looking.

Hand that location to the mount and the two of them can play a short, patient game. Shoot a frame, solve it, compare where you landed against where you wanted to be, nudge the mount to close the gap, and repeat. After two or three rounds the target is sitting exactly where you framed it, no squinting required. This loop, usually called go-to-and-center, is the quiet workhorse underneath almost everything else, framing, polar alignment checks, and finding your way back to the target after the mount flips sides in the middle of the night.

Which makes "Solving…" one of the most important words in the interface, because it is the moment the app is thinking and the user is waiting. When AstroDeck shows that state, the interesting questions are whether it reads as progress or as a hang, and what happens when a solve fails, since a solver that quietly gives up is far worse than one that says, plainly, that it couldn't find the sky and here is what to try next.

Stacking & Calibration

Signal is shy. You coax it out with arithmetic.

A single photo of a galaxy is mostly an honest portrait of your sensor having a bad night. The thing you came for is buried in the grain, barely above it. The fix is not a better single shot. It is a lot of shots, added together with care.

Here is the whole idea in one sentence: the random speckle we call noise is different in every frame, so when you average many frames it partly cancels itself out, while the real light from the sky is in the same place every time and stacks up. Take four times as many frames and the noise drops by about half. Take a hundred and the faint stuff you couldn't even see starts to surface like a photograph developing in a tray.

Stacking averages away the random mess. It does nothing about the errors that show up identically in every frame, and there are three of those worth knowing by name. The move is to photograph your sensor's predictable mistakes once and subtract them from every real frame. The astronomers call these calibration frames, which sounds more ceremonial than it is.

  • Lights
    The actual pictures of the sky. Everything else exists to clean these up.
  • Darks
    Shots with the lens capped. They map the heat glow the sensor makes on its own, so you can subtract it.
  • Flats
    Every lens is a little brighter in the middle and dimmer at the edges, and stray dust on the glass throws soft shadows. A flat is a photo of an evenly lit blank that maps exactly that unevenness, so dividing it out makes the frame uniform again.

    • None of this is glamorous. It is bookkeeping, the kind a careful accountant would respect. But it is the entire difference between a brown smudge and a clean image, and it is the sort of thing software should be quietly handling for you rather than making you remember.

    Where this lands in AstroDeck: the exposure and sequence settings are where someone decides how long each frame runs and how many to collect, and the calibration plan is where darks and flats get scheduled or pulled from a library. If a person has to keep that arithmetic in their own head at 2am, the interface is doing too little. The good version makes the trade visible: more frames, less noise, here's roughly where you are on that curve.

    The Robot Night

    A robot runs the night while you sleep, or eat dinner.

    A full night of imaging is too long and too dull for a human to babysit. So you write down the plan once, hand it to the software, and go inside. A sequence is that plan: find the target, get sharp, take frames, change filters, repeat until dawn, then put everything away.

    Two parts of the plan are worth understanding before you judge any interface that runs it. The first is a chore with a dramatic name. Because the sky appears to rotate overhead, your target drifts from the eastern side of due south to the western side over the course of a night. To follow it, the mount hangs the telescope off one side of its tilted main axis, balanced by a counterweight on the other — a lever. As that lever swings to chase the sky westward, it keeps tipping over until the scope would eventually be pointing down into the tripod legs it stands on. So the mount can't simply keep turning past due south; when the target crosses that line it performs a meridian flip, handing the scope to the other side of the axis: it pauses, swings the whole rig over, re-finds the target, and carries on the same job from a posture that clears the legs. Miss it and best case the night ends early; worst case there's a crunch.

    The second part is the layer wrapped around everything else, and it's the one that matters most when nobody is watching. Clouds roll in and ruin the frames being taken. Dawn arrives and washes out the sky. A roof or dome may need to close before rain. And the single rule that sits above all the others: the scope must never, under any circumstance, swing toward the Sun, because a telescope is an extremely effective device for setting things on fire, your camera sensor very much included. When a safety monitor trips, the sane behavior is to stop imaging, get the equipment to a safe resting position, and wait. At the end of a clean night it does the same thing on purpose and parks itself.

    Where this lands in AstroDeck: the sequencer, the safety monitor, and the park command are the highest-stakes surfaces in the entire app, because they run unattended and the failure modes are expensive. This is where clarity earns its keep. A person glancing at their phone from bed should be able to tell in one second whether the night is fine, whether it flipped on schedule, and what the rig will do the instant something goes wrong. Buried state and ambiguous controls here aren't an annoyance; they're how a $4,000 sensor meets the Sun.
