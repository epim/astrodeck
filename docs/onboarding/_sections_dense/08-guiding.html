Holding Still

Guiding, or a second camera that babysits one star

A mount that tracks the sky is close to right, and close to right is not right when the shutter is open for five minutes.

The earth turns, the sky appears to drift, the mount rotates to cancel the drift. But gears have periodic error and the atmosphere shoves the air around, so over a few minutes small errors accumulate. A star that should be a round point smears into a comma. The errors are measured in arcseconds — the hair-thin angle from the framing section, a coin held a few miles off — and a couple is enough to wreck a frame.

The fix is almost suspiciously simple. A small second camera locks onto one ordinary star and reads it several times a minute, asking the same dull question: did it move? When the star slips a hair left, the guide camera tells the mount to nudge a hair right. The graph below is that conversation.

One deliberate exception to all the holding-still. Between exposures the system shoves the whole frame a few pixels sideways on purpose — a dither (watch the box hop). It reads like sabotage, undoing the precision you just paid for. The point: a hot pixel or fixed smudge stays put on the sensor while the stars land somewhere new each frame, so the stacker can tell signal from junk and discard the junk.

That error graph is the densest surface in the app and probably the most improvable. A jagged line near zero is good news but doesn't feel like good news at a glance, and a beginner reading RMS 0.62″ has no idea whether to relax or panic. A healthy guiding readout in AstroDeck should make you feel the state — relief, not arithmetic — before you've parsed a single number.
