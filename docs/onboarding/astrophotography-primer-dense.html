The Premise

You are about to photograph a faint smudge from a spinning rock

The target is dim, millions of light-years off, and last did anything interesting before there were primates to notice. To collect enough of its light you hold the shutter open for minutes — often hours. Meanwhile the planet under you keeps turning, so the target slides across the sky the entire time you're trying to hold on it.

That drift is the entire problem in one frame. Everything below exists to cancel it.

The craft is three fights at once. Motion: the sky won't sit still. Noise: the signal is faint and the sensor adds static of its own. Focus: a star is a point source, and points punish error. You win by bolting together a stack of boxes — the imaging train. Three boxes do the work; two more earn their place later.

These boxes ship from different vendors and were never designed to talk to each other — the old workflow was five apps, alt-tabbed in the cold at 2am. AstroDeck is the brain that drives the whole train from one screen. The rest of this guide tours what that brain coordinates — each box, the state it exposes, and the one screen where a wrong control placement strands you in the cold.

Pointing the Mount

The sky moves, so the mount moves back

The sky appears to turn only because the Earth turns under it, around one fixed axis. Align one of the mount's axes with that axis, spin a single motor at the matching speed in the opposite direction, and the apparent motion cancels exactly. A star that should slide off the sensor sits still instead.

That alignment is what an equatorial mount is built for: it tilts so one axis runs parallel to the Earth's. Holding a target then costs one motor turning slowly at the sidereal rate — one revolution per sidereal day, about 23h 56m. Below, the frame drifts the way the real sky drifts; flip tracking on and the amber star locks to the crosshair while the rest stops sliding.

The amber star is the one you asked the mount to hold. With tracking on it stops dead — and so does every other star, all carried by that one motor.

You name the point to hold with two numbers — like latitude and longitude, but painted onto the rotating dome of the sky, so they stay constant as the night runs.

RA — Right Ascension

Sky-longitude

Position around the celestial sphere — a fixed label that names the target all night. The RA-axis motor turns at sidereal rate; that constant motion is what keeps this fixed number fixed.

Dec — Declination

Sky-latitude

Degrees north or south of the celestial equator. Once you're on target, this barely moves.

Tracking is excellent and never perfect — periodic gear error, a hair of misalignment — so over a long exposure the star still drifts a few pixels. That residual is why guiding exists, covered later. For now, the control surface is three verbs: slew to point, track on/off to hold, go-to to send the mount to a named target by its coordinates. They live in AstroDeck's mount panel, where two facts have to be glanceable in the dark — where it's pointed, and whether it's holding or drifting — because a mount that silently stopped tracking looks identical to one that didn't.

Aiming the Spin Axis

Polar alignment: aim the axis at the pole, or everything smears

The cancel-the-sky trick has one precondition: the mount's spin axis must point at the exact pivot the sky turns around — the celestial pole, which in the north sits about 0.7° from Polaris. Aim there and the sky holds. Aim a little off and the mount spends the night rotating around the wrong center, smearing every star in a way no single exposure recovers.

The failure mode, while merely tracking, is drift: the field slides steadily in one direction, and each star smears into a short, near-straight streak, all roughly parallel. Longer exposure, longer streak. Both outcomes below, each star drawing its own path over a few minutes — parallel smears left, held points right.

Same sky, same exposure. The only variable is where the spin axis points.

A guider — a second camera that pins one star and erases drift in real time — comes later and works well, which raises the obvious question: if it cancels the smear, why align at all? Because it can only pin one star. With the axis still off, the rest of the field rotates around that pinned point over the night — field rotation, the residual that survives guiding. Good polar alignment keeps that rotation negligible. You don't eyeball Polaris through a hole anymore: a routine (three-point, or a drift test) photographs the sky and plate-solves the frames — reads the star pattern to compute exactly where the scope points — then reports your axis error as a direction and a distance. You correct it with two mechanical knobs: altitude (up/down) and azimuth (left/right). The standard readout is a bullseye: your error is a dot off-center, and you turn knobs to walk it in.

Sharpness, Measured

A star should be a point, not a marshmallow

A star is far enough away to be a true point source — zero angular size at any aperture you'll own. Focus is the job of making the optics agree, collapsing each star to the smallest, brightest dot they can form. Miss it and the same star bloats into a soft pale blob: same sky, worse photo.

A slightly soft star and a sharp one look identical to a human eye at 2am, so you stop trusting eyeballs and trust a number. The standard one is HFR — half-flux radius, the radius enclosing half the star's total light, in pixels. Smaller is sharper. The whole game is driving HFR down and keeping it there.

Focus won't hold: it drifts. As the night cools, the tube's metal contracts a few microns, the focal point shifts, and sharp stars quietly puff back up. So you refocus periodically — a chore built for automation, which is where this goes: re-running the V-curve on a temperature or time trigger, watching HFR, moving the focuser with no human in the loop.

In AstroDeck's focus panel, the HFR readout is the protagonist, not the buttons around it. The user is hunting a minimum; the two control flows — a manual nudge and the full V-curve run — exist to make that number small and say, plainly, when it is.

The Sensor's Self-Inflicted Lies

Cold sensors lie less

A sensor is supposed to report the light that lands on it. It also reports light that never arrived — a faint static it generates on its own, just by being a warm electronic thing. That's dark current, and on a long exposure it speckles the frame with noise the sky never put there.

Two things drive it: heat and time. Dark current roughly doubles every 6–8°C, and it accumulates the whole exposure. Astrophotography is staring at one faint thing for minutes per frame, so you can't cut the exposure. You cut the temperature.

For anyone the animation skips: the grainy frame settles clean as the sensor falls +18°C → −10°C, and the status flips from pulsing amber "cooling" to steady green "at set-point."

The hardware is a thermoelectric cooler (a Peltier) bolted to the sensor, holding a chosen set-point — say −10°C — all night. The set-point matters more than raw cold: you cancel the leftover speckle by subtracting reference dark frames, and that subtraction only works if the darks were shot at the same temperature. A repeatable −10°C beats a colder number you can't hit twice.

A cooler also has a floor — roughly 35–45°C below ambient — so on a warm night the number you typed may be unreachable. The indicator has to report where it actually is, not where you asked it to go.

In AstroDeck, the cooler box answers one question the user asks all night: cooling, at set-point, or off? That state has to read at a glance and stay honest about the in-between, instead of leaving the user doing subtraction on a raw temperature.

Pointing & Composition

How much sky fits, and where the target lands

Before focus, cooling, and hours of exposure comes a creative question: what's in the shot? A telescope is a lens with a fixed crop. Your only compositional moves are how wide that crop is and where you aim it. Get them wrong and you've spent the night perfectly photographing the corner of something.

Field of view is focal length against sensor size. Long focal length = tight crop, right for a small distant galaxy, hopeless for anything big. Short focal length = wide field that swallows a sprawling nebula but renders each object small. Underneath sits pixel scale (arcseconds per pixel), the detail dial, with a sweet spot around 1–2″/px. The UI's job is keeping the user in that band, not handing them a knob they'll crank to the max.

Drag focal length and the amber frame widens or crops over the nebula. FOV reads in degrees; the full Moon is ~0.5° across, so 2.3° is about four-and-a-half Moons wide. Hit plate-solve & center and a scan line sweeps the sky, then the frame slides to put the target dead-center.

Aiming feels like magic and is really bookkeeping. The mount points where it thinks the target is, the camera grabs a quick frame, and the software plate-solves it: matches the star pattern against a catalog, derives exactly where the scope is actually pointed, and nudges until the target sits where you framed it. No star-hopping, no "a little to the left." When a target won't fit even the widest sensible frame, you shoot it as a multi-panel mosaic and stitch the tiles later.

On AstroDeck's framing screen, the sky atlas does two jobs: a true-to-scale preview of what the sensor will capture, and a way to place that rectangle with intent. The open UX question is how a wide field, a tight crop, and a multi-tile mosaic all live in one preview without the user ever picturing arcseconds in their head.

Framing the Shot

Camera angle, where rotation becomes composition

The telescope decides what you point at. The rotation of the camera behind it decides how that thing sits in the frame.

A sensor is a rectangle and the sky doesn't care which way you hold it. Spin the camera and the same galaxy streaks corner to corner or lies flat across the middle — nothing about the target changed, you rotated the rectangle. That angle is the position angle, and it's as much a part of the photograph as exposure or focus.

Two ways to set it. Loosen a clamp and turn the camera by hand in the dark, then lock it close to right. Or a motorized rotator drives it to a number you asked for. Either way you don't know you nailed it until the sky tells you — through plate-solving, which reads an exposure and reports the angle the camera actually landed at, to a fraction of a degree.

That readout matters most in a mosaic, several tiles meant to line up into one larger picture. If panel three is two degrees off panels one and two, the seam shows, and no amount of later wrangling fully hides a crooked join. Planned angle and actual angle have to agree.

So at AstroDeck's rotation control, the question is how clearly it shows the gap between the angle you asked for and the angle you got. One number is a setting. Two side by side, with the difference called out, is something you can steer by.

Holding Still

Guiding, or a second camera that babysits one star

A mount that tracks the sky is close to right, and close to right is not right when the shutter is open for five minutes.

The earth turns, the sky appears to drift, the mount rotates to cancel the drift. But gears have periodic error and the atmosphere shoves the air around, so over a few minutes small errors accumulate. A star that should be a round point smears into a comma. The errors are measured in arcseconds — the hair-thin angle from the framing section, a coin held a few miles off — and a couple is enough to wreck a frame.

The fix is almost suspiciously simple. A small second camera locks onto one ordinary star and reads it several times a minute, asking the same dull question: did it move? When the star slips a hair left, the guide camera tells the mount to nudge a hair right. The graph below is that conversation.

One deliberate exception to all the holding-still. Between exposures the system shoves the whole frame a few pixels sideways on purpose — a dither (watch the box hop). It reads like sabotage, undoing the precision you just paid for. The point: a hot pixel or fixed smudge stays put on the sensor while the stars land somewhere new each frame, so the stacker can tell signal from junk and discard the junk.

That error graph is the densest surface in the app and probably the most improvable. A jagged line near zero is good news but doesn't feel like good news at a glance, and a beginner reading RMS 0.62″ has no idea whether to relax or panic. A healthy guiding readout in AstroDeck should make you feel the state — relief, not arithmetic — before you've parsed a single number.

Knowing Where You Are

Plate solving: the app working out exactly where it's pointed

A telescope knows where you told it to point. Whether it actually got there is a separate, more optimistic question.

The fix is a content-addressed lookup on the sky. The app shoots a quick frame, detects the stars in it, and measures the geometry between them — distances and angles for groups of four, encoded into a hash. It queries an all-sky catalog for that exact hash. Bright star patterns are effectively unique, so one hit pins down three things at once: where the scope points (RA/Dec), how the camera is rotated, and the image scale (arcseconds per pixel). GPS for a telescope, built entirely out of looking at the picture it just took.

Feed that solved position back to the mount and you get a closed loop: shoot, solve, measure the offset from where you wanted to be, slew to correct, repeat. Two or three iterations and the target lands dead center, typically within a few arcseconds. This loop — go-to-and-center — sits under almost everything: framing, polar-alignment routines, and re-finding the target after the mount flips to the other side of the sky mid-night.

Which makes "Solving…" one of the load-bearing words in the UI: the app is busy and the user is blocked on it. For AstroDeck, the real questions are whether that state reads as progress or as a hang, and what the failure path looks like. A solve fails on clouds, a smeared frame, or a wrong image scale — and a solver that gives up in silence is far worse than one that says it couldn't find the sky and names the next thing to try.

Stacking & Calibration

Signal is shy. You coax it out with arithmetic.

A single frame of a galaxy is mostly a portrait of your sensor having a bad night. The target sits buried in grain, a hair above it. The fix isn't a better exposure — it's many exposures, averaged with care.

Noise is random, so it lands in a different place every frame and averages toward zero. The galaxy's light hits the same pixels every time and accumulates. Signal-to-noise scales with the square root of the frame count: 4× the frames halves the noise, 100× drops it tenfold, and detail you couldn't see at all surfaces like a print coming up in the developer tray.

Averaging kills the random component. It does nothing about errors that repeat identically in every frame, and three of those have names. The fix: photograph the sensor's predictable mistakes and subtract them. These are calibration frames.

  • Lights
    The actual sky frames. Everything else exists to clean these up.
  • Darks
    Same exposure and temperature, shutter closed. Maps the sensor's own thermal glow so you can subtract it.
  • Flats
    A shot of an evenly lit blank field. It records the optics' falloff — brighter center, darker edges (vignetting) — and the soft donuts from dust on the glass. Dividing it out flattens the frame.

    • It's bookkeeping, not glamour. It's also the entire gap between a brown smudge and a clean image, and exactly the kind of thing software should run for you instead of making you memorize it.

    Where this lands in AstroDeck: exposure and sequence settings decide sub length and frame count; the calibration plan schedules darks and flats or pulls them from a matched library. If someone has to hold that math in their head at 2am, the UI is doing too little. The good version surfaces the trade live — more frames, less noise, here's where you are on the curve.

    The Robot Night

    A robot runs the night while you sleep, or eat dinner.

    A full night of imaging is too long and too dull to babysit. You write the plan once, hand it to the software, and go inside. A sequence is that plan: find the target, focus, shoot frames, swap filters, repeat until dawn, then stow everything.

    Two parts of the plan decide whether any interface running it is any good. The first is a chore with a dramatic name. The sky appears to rotate, so your target drifts from east of due south to west across the night. The mount tracks it by hanging the scope off one side of its tilted polar axis, balanced by a counterweight on the other — a lever. Keep swinging that lever west and the tube eventually points down into its own tripod. So the mount can't cross due south on the same side; when the target hits that line it runs a meridian flip: pause, swing the rig to the other side of the axis, re-find the target, resume from a posture that clears the legs. Miss it and best case the night ends early; worst case, a crunch.

    The second part wraps everything else, and it's the part that earns its keep when nobody's watching: the safety layer. Clouds ruin frames mid-exposure. Dawn washes the sky out. A roll-off roof or dome may need to close before rain. And the rule above all others — the scope must never slew toward the Sun, because a telescope is a superb instrument for setting things on fire, your sensor included. When a safety monitor trips, the correct behavior is fixed: stop imaging, slew to a safe resting position, wait. A clean night ends the same way on purpose — the rig parks itself.

    Where this lands in AstroDeck: the sequencer, the safety monitor, and the park command are the highest-stakes surfaces in the app — they run unattended, and the failure modes are expensive. A half-awake person checking their phone from bed should read three things in one second: is the night fine, did it flip on schedule, and what does the rig do the instant something breaks. Buried state and ambiguous controls here aren't friction; they're how a $4,000 sensor meets the Sun.

    What AstroDeck owes this night: twelve surfaces that handed off cleanly without you re-deciding anything you already decided. The cooler page, the polar-align solver, plate-solve-and-center, the V-curve focuser, the rotator readout, the guiding graph, the sequencer, the safety monitor, the park button — each is one beat above, and the seams between them are where a night quietly dies. A flip that doesn't re-solve, a refocus that never fires, a park that forgets the Sun. The job of the UI is to make those handoffs legible enough that you can be asleep for most of them.
