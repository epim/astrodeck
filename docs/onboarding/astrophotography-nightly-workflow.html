AstroDeck · Onboarding

A night, step by step

You write the software that runs the telescope. You have never run one. This is the night an astrophotographer actually has — ten steps in the order they happen — and what AstroDeck is doing at each. Scroll; the diagrams replay as you go.

Each step does→ facts→ BREAKS→ UI — what you do, the numbers that matter, what goes wrong if you skip it, and where it lives in AstroDeck.

01 Setting Up

Level it, point it north

Plant the tripod on firm ground, level it, and aim the mount's rotation axis at true (geographic) north.

Level
A bubble level on the mount head; off-level skews everything downstream.
North
TRUE north, not magnetic — they differ by the local magnetic declination, up to tens of degrees.
Latitude
Set the altitude wedge to your site latitude so the axis tilts up toward the pole.
Why north
An equatorial mount tracks by spinning one axis; that axis must point at the celestial pole, which sits due north in the northern hemisphere.

Coarse setup by hand; the software refines it later.

A tilted or mis-aimed mount makes polar alignment and every GoTo start from a bad reference — you fight it the rest of the night.

AstroDeck assumes a roughly polar-aligned mount and reads your site latitude/longitude from config (site) to compute where everything is in the sky.

This is the one step no API call can do for you.

02 Powering On

Power on, boot, connect

Bring up power to the mount, camera, focuser, filter wheel and guider, then start AstroDeck and let it connect to each device.

Order
Power the USB hub, dew heaters and mount first, then the host computer running AstroDeck — peripherals up before the bus enumerates.
Devices
An imaging camera, mount, focuser, filter wheel and guider: 5 roles, each driven by a separate driver.
Backends
AstroDeck reaches gear through a backend — a simulator, Alpaca, or NINA — so the same UI drives real or fake hardware.
Profiles
A saved profile maps each role to a device and auto-connects on boot, so a known rig comes up in one step.
Mountok
Cameraok
Focuserok
Wheelok
Guiderok
backend simulator profile backyard-rig

Each device connects independently; the session waits for the roles it needs.

A device that fails to connect — or connects to the wrong backend — stalls the run before the first frame.

AstroDeck shows a backend + profile picker and a tri-state connection status per role; viewers get a read-only view with no control.

The simulator backend connects instantly and never clouds over.

03 Cooling

Cool the camera

Run the camera's thermoelectric cooler down to a stable set-point so thermal noise stays low and, just as important, repeatable.

Ambient
Uncooled, the sensor sits near ambient (sim: +12.3 °C).
Set-point
A typical target is −10 °C, held to a fixed delta all night.
Dark current
Sensor noise roughly halves for every ~6 °C cooler.
Headroom
Pick a set-point the cooler can hold (~70–80% power) so it stays locked as the night warms or cools.

Drag the set-point — the grain is dark current you can't fully subtract away.

A warm sensor buries faint nebulosity in thermal noise; cooling too far or too fast can frost the sensor with condensation.

AstroDeck's monitor shows live sensor temperature and cooler power %, and the sequence can warm the cooler gently at the end of the night (warm_cooler_when_done).

−10 °C isn't sacred; it just has to be repeatable, so your dark frames match.

04 First Slew

Slew to a bright star

Send the mount to a known bright star — often Polaris or another northern object — to check pointing and give focus something to work on.

GoTo
The mount slews using its internal pointing model.
The miss
An uncalibrated model lands the star off-centre, sometimes outside the frame entirely.
Plate-solve + sync
Image the field, solve where the scope actually points, and sync the model to truth.
Park
A known home reference (the sim parks near the pole at Dec +89.5°).

First GoTo lands close, not centred; the plate-solve fixes the rest.

Trust an unsynced model and the target sits outside the sensor — you expose empty sky and never notice.

MountView has a Slew Pad, a Pointing panel (RA / Dec / Alt / Az / State) and a Target Catalog; a plate-solve syncs the model to where it truly points.

The mount is confident and wrong; the camera is how it learns.

05 Focusing

Find focus

Move the focuser until stars are as tight as possible — measured by the software, not eyeballed.

HFR
Focus quality is the star's half-flux radius; smaller is sharper.
V-curve
HFR vs focuser position traces a V; autofocus samples both arms and solves for the minimum.
Per filter
Each filter comes to focus at a slightly different point (filter focus offsets).
Temp drift
Focus shifts as the scope cools, so it refocuses on a temperature delta.

Autofocus samples the V and drives the focuser to its floor.

Soft focus fattens every star and drowns faint detail — no amount of processing puts it back.

FocusView shows a Live Preview, an Autofocus routine (exposure, step size) and a V-Curve · HFR vs Position plot; Automation can autofocus every N frames, apply filter offsets, and refocus on a temperature delta.

06 Polar Alignment

Three-point polar alignment

Refine the mount-axis-to-pole error by plate-solving three points along a slow rotation in RA — no need to actually see the pole star.

Why
Tracking spins one axis; if that axis isn't on the true pole, the field slowly rotates and stars trail.
Three points
Solving the sky at three RA positions fixes the rotation axis relative to the true celestial pole.
No Polaris needed
It's computed from any solvable star field, not a sight line to Polaris.
Result
Azimuth and altitude error in arcminutes; you turn the mount's alt/az knobs to drive them toward zero.

Three solved points define the spin axis; the reticle shows how far it misses the pole.

Skip it and long exposures show field rotation — stars trail in little arcs even with guiding running perfectly.

AstroDeck drives a TPPA routine (via NINA) and publishes one polar reading — az / alt / total error in arcmin — to a live reticle, with pause/resume.

The old way needed a clear sightline to Polaris; plate-solving doesn't — good news for everyone south of the equator, behind a tree, or otherwise invited to check their privilege.

07 Guiding

Guide out the drift (PHD2)

A second camera watches one star and sends tiny correction pulses to the mount, cancelling the small errors that tracking and polar alignment leave behind.

Loop
Measure the guide star's offset, pulse the mount to push it back, repeat every ~1–3 s.
RMS
Guiding quality is the residual error in arcseconds; smaller means rounder stars.
Dither
Shift a few pixels between frames so stacking rejects hot pixels and walking noise.
PHD2
The de-facto guiding engine AstroDeck drives.

Each pulse pushes the star back toward the crosshair; the trace is the residual it can't catch.

Without guiding, the field slowly walks during long sub-exposures and stars smear into ovals.

AstroDeck reports live guide RMS and exposes a dither setting in the sequence Automation.

08 Guiding Optics

Why the guide scope isn't the main scope

Guiding gets its own small, short-focal-length scope and camera, separate from the imaging train.

Wide field
A short guide scope sees a wide patch of sky, so it almost always finds a usable guide star.
Main scope
Long focal length, narrow field, tuned for the target — a poor place to go hunting for guide stars.
Cost vs flexure
A piggybacked guide scope is cheap and light, but any flex between the two tubes shows up as drift.
Alternative
An off-axis guider (OAG) picks a star out of the main scope's own light, removing flexure, at the cost of a dim, cramped field.

Wide guide field, narrow imaging field — same sky, different jobs.

Differential flexure makes guiding look perfect on its own star while the main image quietly drifts.

AstroDeck resolves the guider role and its guide camera separately from the imaging camera.

The guide camera is the cheapest, ugliest sensor in the rig, and it never has to look pretty.

09 Filter Wheel

Filters, and how they combine

A mono camera shoots through one filter at a time; you stack each channel separately, then combine them into a color image.

Wheel
A motorized wheel rotates filters in front of the sensor (sim: a 7×36 mm wheel).
Broadband
L R G B: red/green/blue build natural color, and luminance adds brightness and detail.
Narrowband
Ha OIII SII isolate single emission lines; they reject light pollution and moonlight but pass little light, so subs run long.
False color
Narrowband channels are assigned to RGB by choice; the "Hubble palette" is SHO (SII→R, Ha→G, OIII→B).

Toggle channels — RGB build natural color and L adds the punch; the SHO trio each lights different structure into a false-color palette.

Forget to refocus per filter or to match each filter's exposure to its throughput and a channel comes back soft or starved — the composite is unusable.

AstroDeck moves the wheel by slot (/api/filterwheel/position), names the slots L R G B Ha OIII SII, and applies per-filter focus offsets in Automation.

The colors of a narrowband image are a decision, not a measurement.

10 Planning

Plan the night, then let it run

Choose targets that are high and dark during your imaging window, set exposures per filter, and queue a sequence that runs unattended.

Altitude
Image a target near its highest point (the meridian), where its light cuts through the least air.
Meridian flip
A German equatorial mount must swap sides as the target crosses the meridian (when the hour angle HA = LST − RA reaches 0).
Budget
Total integration = sub length × frame count, split across filters.
Safety
The sequence handles refocus, dither, the flip, and aborts on its own.

Best signal near the meridian; the run flips the mount as the target crosses it.

Pick a target too low (or behind a tree), or run past the meridian without a flip, and you lose the subs — or crash the mount into the pier.

SequenceView holds the Plan (frames, integration), a Targets list, and Automation (meridian flip, HFR spike rejection, dither, filter offsets).

Most imagers plan before dark; it sits last here because it's the part you keep tuning all night.
